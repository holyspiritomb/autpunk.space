I am no stranger to medical procedures. I didn't have adequate access to medical care as a child, so I wasn't diagnosed with any of my disabilities until I was in my 20s. I had to learn how to advocate for myself at the doctor's office as an adult. The basic tenets of medical self advocacy are

- My doctors work for me, not the other way around.
- I have the right to refuse any part of a medical examination, including being weighed.
- I have the right to disagree with a doctor's advice if it won't work for me.
- I deserve to have a doctor who doesn't become offended when I say a piece of advice won't work for me.
- I know my own experiences better than anyone else, even doctors. No one else has the ability to determine whether I'm uncomfortable or in pain because they are not having my experiences.
- I deserve to be treated with respect and taken seriously.

It is my opinion that the best doctor is one who is willing to learn from patients and is aware of the limits of their knowledge. Such doctors can be difficult to find, so it is necessary, especially for us disabled and chronically ill people, to advovate for ourselves in order to get the medical care we need. And even if you have one of the good ones, your doctor will probably not immediately recognize underdiagnosed conditions unless you explicitly talk about them at an appointment, because doctors are trained to think of more commonly-diagnosed conditions first via the metaphor "If you hear hoofbeats, think horses, not zebras." (This is why Ehlers-Danlos syndrome advocacy groups often use a zebra as a symbol.)

So here are a few strategies that you may find helpful. Especially if you're a zebra, or anyone who has had an experience of being dismissed or gaslit by a medical provider.

I'm going to order my tips by amount of effort required.

## Bring a Friend

Bringing another adult to doctor appointments is something I do frequently. When you bring a friend to an appointment, their mere presence can increase your confidence. Bringing another adult also helps me stay focused. But it also has an effect on medical professionals.

:::details a story about a time I was The Friend
When a friend was being evaluated for autism a few weeks after I got my paper diagnosis in the same office from a different provider, I was The Friend who came to the final appointment. The specialist I'd seen for my paper diagnosis was very highly educated about how autism manifests in adults, but the specialist in my friend's case was... less knowledgeable, to be polite about it.

At one point the specialist told my friend, "The testing says you're autistic but I just have a gut feeling you're not. Because you have friends." I pointed out, "Your colleague diagnosed me recently and didn't think that having friends meant I couldn't be autistic." The specialist changed tactics and suggested that my friend's autistic traits pertaining to social struggles were instead due to social isolation of being trans^[This is an interesting variation/inversion of the equally bad idea, asserted by Tony Atwood in his 2006 book *The Complete Guide to Asperger's Syndrome*, that autistic people who understand themselves as trans are mistaken because autistic social difficulties prevent understanding what gender Really Means(tm). Based on this idea, autism diagnosis has been used against trans autistic adults in the form of denial of medical transition. But one does not need to Understand(tm) Gender to know such facts about oneself as "I want to have the other common set of reproductive organs" or "I want to have a flat chest" or "I feel more like myself when called she/her." Did I have an "I'm Not Like Other Girls" phase? Yes. Because I didn't have any other language. Now that transmasc people are more visible, there are still lots of butches and lots girls who Aren't Like Other Girls, Tony. And some of them are trans, because trans women are just as varied in gender presentation as cis women, Tony.]. I was not going to let that slide. I pointed out that we knew many other trans people (autistic and not), my friend's social difficulties happened with other trans people, and that I myself am trans AND autistic.

The specialist decided my friend was probably autistic after all, despite having friends amd being trans. I hope it was a learning experience for this specialist.
:::

Even if the friend accompanying you doesn't say anything and is merely present, a medical provider who has been dismissive to you in the past will think twice about responding to you dismissively. Because now there's a witness who can corroborate your experience.

### Variation: Bring a Man

This recommendation is for people who have the experience of being taken less seriously by medical providers because of not being (or not being regarded as) a man. A doctor is likely to be on their best behavior if the friend you bring to your appointment is a man. Your Man Friend's presence is a signal to the doctor that your symptoms are real, because a Man takes you seriously. This effect happens with doctors of all genders. As one of my friends put it, "Having a man present unlocks additional narrative options."

## Second opinions

Sometimes, there isn't an option other than just firing a specialist and seeking a second opinion.

:::details a story about a bad experience I had with a specialist
The first time I had a gynecologist appointment, other than with the gynecological surgeon who performed my hysterectomy^[My surgeon was awesome and I'd recommend her if she weren't now retired.], I did not have a good experience. I had come because I had developed persistent pain in my abdomen several months after my hysterectomy. I did not have the ability to see my surgeon to diagnose it, and a gastro specialist I'd been seeing had determined after a lot of examination and testing that the pain was unrelated to anything in her specialty.

During the first appointment, the gynecologist told me I was too constipated for her to do an internal exam, which already made me feel bad about myself for having a medical condition. As an additional insult, she sent me home with a can of steel-cut oats and a piece of paper recommending I start every day by drinking four cups of plain warm water (<span i-dinkie-icons:face-with-open-mouth-vomiting />). When I returned to her two weeks later, she was equally dismissive, and after doing an exam and finding nothing out of the ordinary, she sent me on my way. It was the gynecological equivalent of "Your labs are great! What are you complaining about?" (My fellow chronically ill people know what I mean.) There was not a way to get a word in edgewise. I still had the abdominal pain.
:::

I told my primary care doctor about this bad experience, being careful to use a lot of "I" statements and to focus on how this gynecologist was a bad fit for me. My PCP had been the one to recommend this gynecologist and I did not want to come across as insulting my PCP's judgment. I asked my PCP to refer me to a different gynecologist for a second opinion. I had a significantly better experience with the second gynecologist.
