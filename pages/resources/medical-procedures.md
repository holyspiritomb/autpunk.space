I am no stranger to medical procedures. I didn't have adequate access to medical care as a child, so I wasn't diagnosed with any of my disabilities until I was in my 20s. I had to learn how to advocate for myself at the doctor's office as an adult. The basic tenets of medical self advocacy are

- My doctors work for me, not the other way around.
- I have the right to refuse any part of a medical examination, including being weighed.
- I have the right to disagree with a doctor's advice if it won't work for me.
- I deserve to have a doctor who doesn't become offended when I say a piece of advice won't work for me.

This is especially important to me is because I live with children who have had various medical procedures before one year old.

:::details Content warning: I describe a child's medical trauma of attempted IV placement

When the older child I live with was four months old, they had a mass behind one eye that was getting larger. The mass ended up being benign and treatable with medication alone, but the first steps of the process for diagnosis involved admission to a hospital.

Upon admission to a hospital crib, a nurse came to place an IV. It wasn't in preparation for surgery; it was just the hospital's routine to give every admitted patient IV fluids. This nurse spent at least 20 minutes attempting to place an IV on our baby at different sites, while our baby screamed in pain and terror the entire time. We didn't intervene sooner because we were afflicted by that altered mental state of "Is this really actually happening?" combined with "an authority figure is doing something." It took immense willpower for one of us to demand for the nurse to stop. And since then, we have intervened much sooner in instances where the children we care for are in pain, and we advocate for everything possible to be done to lessen the pain of a necessary procedure.
:::

If you are a parent, I hope this page will be useful for making common medical procedures less painful for your child. It is my hope that sharing information about making medical procedures hurt less will empower parents to intervene in the event that their child is subjected to (or about to be subjected to) unnecessary amounts of pain during a medical procedure.

## Blood pressure measurements

I was 37 years old when I learned that having blood pressure measured with a cuff on the upper arm, when done correctly, was supposed to be uncomfortable but wasn't supposed to hurt. I had experienced having my blood pressure measured on my upper arm as painful for my entire life, without exception. (Which might be medically significant, since I learned it from an acquaintance with fibromyalgia, and also from the Fibromyalgia subreddit^[There are SO MANY POSTS on [r/Fibromyalgia](https://www.reddit.com/r/Fibromyalgia/search/?q=blood+pressure+cuff+hurts+when+inflated&cId=4bb04abd-1940-4724-b6b4-9dab91985f01&iId=f9b74d9e-22e2-41a5-a1eb-afb0f104afb0) where people are like "What do you mean having blood pressure measured isn't supposed to hurt?? It always hurts!"]. I don't have a fibromyalgia diagnosis yet.)

Then I learned that blood pressure can be measured on the forearm or wrist. And for the very first time in my life, I had my blood pressure measured and it didn't hurt. The squeezing on my forearm was uncomfortable, but the important thing is that it was painless. If you experience upper-arm blood pressure measurements as painful, you don't have to just take it!

:::info Getting blood pressure measured TLDR
- Ask for the provider to manually measure via your forearm or wrist
- Tell the provider to document it in your chart that upper-arm blood pressure measurement is painful for you (this is medically significant information)
:::

## Needle procedures

I have different advice based on the type of injection, because injecting into tissue is different than directly accessing a blood vessel. But one tip I have that applies to all needle procedures I describe is to use the thinnest needle that can do the job. I learned from handsewing that a thinner needle will pass through material more easily than a thicker one, and this applies to the human body just as it applies to fabric.

### Intramuscular (IM) injections

Examples:
- Vaccines administered into your shoulder
- Depo-Provera injected into upper gluteal muscle
- Testosterone cypionate injected into upper outer thigh

Intramuscular injections are meant to dispense medication into muscle. They use the largest needles you'll encounter outside of specialist offices because they have to go so deep. Many vaccines are given intramuscularly, and it is common for testosterone self-injection to be intramuscular. I have particular experience with injecting testosterone into my own thigh, but my suggestions also apply to receiving an intramuscular injection performed by someone else.

An intramuscular injection's pain is inversely proportional to the speed of needle insertion. Faster insertion = less pain because a lot of the potential pain comes from the muscle spasming as the needle enters.

One easy way to reduce or eliminate pain during an intramuscular injection is applying ice to the injection site for 5 minutes to numb the area. This won't stop soreness from developing afterwards, but it will stop pain sensations while the needle is being inserted.

:::info Intramuscular injection TLDR
- Ice the injection site for at least five minutes prior to the injection.
- Allow the alcohol on the skin to dry before the injection to prevent a burning sensation on insertion.
- Tell the provider not to give a warning or countdown (and don't watch) to prevent you from subconsciously tensing up.
- Self-injection tip: Just jam the needle in as fast as you can, at a right angle to the skin surface. Once it's in, you've gotten past the most potentially painful part and you can dispense the medicine and remove the needle at your own pace.
:::

### Subcutaneous injections

As of this writing, I am receiving allergy shots, and in my case that means 3 subcutaneous injections per week, one after the other^[One for pollens (trees, grasses and weeds), one for molds, and the third for cats, dogs and dust.]. Some vaccines are given as subcutaneous injections. Testosterone can be administered subcutaneously as well, and this is a popular choice because the amount of pain that's possible during self-injection is less than with IM^[In my opinion, IM self-injection into the thigh also has the potential for being completely painless even without numbing the injection site. YMMV.], and you are less limited in your choice of injection sites. 

Subcutaneous injections are meant to diffuse into the layer of fat under the skin. The needles are shorter and thinner than those used for IM injections, and the injection technique is to grab a roll of fat and go in at an angle. Since the injection is into tissue and it's shallower than an IM injection, icing the area will work well here. You can also ask for numbing cream to be applied to the injection site.

:::info Subcutaneous injection TLDR
- Ice the injection site for at least five minutes prior to the injection.
- Numbing cream can be applied to the injection site ~30 minutes beforehand.
- Allow the alcohol on the skin to dry before the injection to prevent a burning sensation on insertion.
- Tell the provider not to give a warning or countdown (and don't watch).
:::


### Fingerpricks

For medical tests that require only a drop or two of blood, there are special fingerprick machines that automatically jab and retract a tiny lancet. The finger is then squeezed to get a drop of blood onto a test strip, and the test strip is then inserted into a machine to get a fast reading. This is a common way for people with diabetes to test their blood glucose.

Because icing a finger would make blood vessels contract, it can't be used here. So numbing cream is going to be your best bet for reducing pain.

:::info Fingerprick TLDR
- Ask for numbing cream to be applied to the chosen fingertip(s) ~15 minutes beforehand
- DO NOT USE ICE
:::


### Blood draws

The first time I had a real blood draw that wasn't a pinprick (e.g. more than a drop of blood), I almost fainted. Only the nurse's quick thinking (to place some strong-smelling chemical under my nose) prevented me from actually passing out.

As with fingerpricks, ice can't be used to numb an area, but numbing cream absolutely can! Additionally, asking for the smallest possible needle will reduce pain.

When I have a blood draw coming up, I start hydrating extra hard for at least a day beforehand, especially if I must be fasting. This makes blood vessels wider and easier to find for a medical provider.

:::info Blood draws
- Start hydrating extra for a day or so before a blood draw, so your veins are good and fat.
- Ask for numbing cream to be applied to the site beforehand.
- Allow the alcohol on the site to dry.
- Ask for the smallest possible needle to be used.
- If fainting is a risk for you, ask to sit in the chair they use for people at risk of fainting.
- Don't watch.
:::

### IV placement

Examples of times it's used:
- General anaesthesia for surgery
- Sedation for colonoscopy or wisdom tooth removal^[Because I find oral procedures relaxing, I chose not to be sedated for wisdom tooth removal.]
- Delivery of contrast dyes for CT scans
- Emergency hydration in a patient who can't keep liquids down
- Some chemotherapy^[Chemotherapy drugs are extremely harsh on blood vessels (good for killing tumors, not so good for everything else), so for drugs that are given intravenously over long periods of time, it is common to install a temporary port for access to a large vein for the duration of treatment.]

It is a common misconception that the needle used for IV placement stays inside you. The needle is in fact only used to create a passageway for a flexible tube to be inserted for fluid and medication delivery, and it is then removed. Nothing sharp stays inside your vein.

The best way to prevent pain from getting an IV placed is not to have one placed at all. Ask if it's absolutely necessary, and if the answer is "no", you can refuse without it being used against you.

But IVs are necessary for surgery and some other procedures. I have found in my particular experience that an IV attached to the back of my hand is less painful to place than one in the crook of my elbow. I happen to have very veiny hands, I guess?

Hydration is beneficial for the same reasons as in blood draws, if you know you're having a medical procedure that requires an IV.

:::info IV placement TLDR
- If an IV isn't absolutely necessary, you can refuse.
- Drinking extra fluids two days leading up to a procedure requiring IV placement will make it easier.
- Numbing cream can be applied to the site beforehand.
- Ask for the smallest possible needle to be used for insertion.
- If the provider can't insert an IV after two tries, ask for another provider to do it.
:::

## Dental and orthodontic procedures

### Palate expansion and other orthodontic treatment

:::details Content warning: I relate an experience of orthodontic malpractice
I learned as an adult that the purpose of a palate expander was not in fact to break bone, and that it is deeply unethical to do palate expansion without any pain control on an adolescent whose hard palate bones have fused. Palate expansion is not a comfortable experience for kids, but it is meant to stretch the cartilage between the two sides of the hard palate before it ossifies in adolescence.

The first time (and every other time) my palate expander's key was turned, there were audible cracks and it was excruciatingly painful. It was a degree of pain unmatched by any other experience of my life until I had mesenteric adenitis in my early 30s^[Mesenteric adenitis is a "benign" condition where the lymph nodes in your abdominal wall swell up and hurt sharply, and the pain is unaffected by OTC pain relief methods. My lymph nodes were freaking out because I was two months into recovering from major abdominal surgery (hysterectomy), and my body was still freaking out about having fewer organs. My misbehaving lymph nodes calmed down after a few more months 🙃.]. I was 16 and my palate had ossified. My orthodontist was unresponsive to my reports of pain and never offered any pain control or pain relief advice. He described me as "dramatic" for how I reacted to repeatedly having a bone broken.
:::

Palate expansion is the orthodontic process by which the upper jaw is widened. It is used to treat dental crowding and sometimes to increase the airway, and there are many methods but all involve some amount of pain and soreness. When I learned in adulthood that what happened to me was at the very least deeply unethical (and at worst, torture), I wanted to prevent it from happening to any other people.

If you are told your child would benefit from palate expansion, it is imperative that before installing any appliance, the orthodontist X-rays the child's mouth to determine whether the hard palate is fused, especially if your child has started puberty. The way all palate expanders work is stretching the cartilage that separates the sides of the hard palate. Puberty is when that cartilage starts turning into bone. If that cartilage has ossified, there is a surgical procedure for re-separating it prior to using palate expansion appliances.

:::info Advocating for your kid re: orthodontic treatment
- If palate expansion is recommended, ask for X-ray confirmation that the hard palate isn't fused.
- Directly ask the orthodontist about pain control during orthodontic appointments. If they brush you off, get a different orthodontist.
- Be in the room while your kid is getting their appliances adjusted.
- Interrupt the doctor if it seems like your kid needs a momentary break.
:::
