*Adapted from [a thread I wrote on Bluesky](https://bsky.app/profile/spiritomb.bsky.social/post/3lciu4fzl322b)*

I messaged a new follower from today whose description included "antitheist" that if my posting about religion is not what they signed up for, they can unfollow/mute/block with no hard feelings.

They blocked me, and I feel good that this happened without any surprises or retraumatization.

I think that the subset of atheists who come to describe themselves as anti-theists are likely to be carrying some kind of religious trauma.

In my own life, I know I wouldn't have been able to be happy for people who enjoy being Christians until I was respected by my family as not-Christian.

I was identifying as atheist during the part of my life that I regularly went with a friend to her Catholic church's Spanish services. My friend respected that I as an atheist (who had deliberately rejected Catholicism & Christianity) found her church meditative. She understood me.

This friend never tried to convert me back to Catholicism. That deep respect from her was necessary in order for me to develop curiosity about what Catholicism looks like in Mexican immigrant communities, compared to the Polish-immigrant Catholicism I'd grown up with.

My parents were, to put it mildly, *disappointed* in me for not being Confirmed as Catholic, despite that they didn't even go to mass. I was nonreligious afterward. So I kept it a secret when I attended a classmate's funeral mass when I was in high school, which I did to show solidarity with bereaved classmates who knew him better.

When I was 13, I reasoned that reciting the Nicene Creed (a series of "I believe" statements that summarize Christian theology) during my Confirmation would require me to lie, and lying was bad, and wouldn't my family rather I not lie about my beliefs? 

They only begrudgingly let me leave Catholicism.

Christianity is a proselytizing creedal religion. Converting others is encouraged, and there's a set of required beliefs for being a Christian. Bringing someone back to the faith who had left is considered an *especially* good deed in Christianity.

I didn't trust my relatives to respect my atheism.

My mother especially did not respect me during the part of my life where I was atheist and nonreligious. She also REALLY didn't like my briefly trying out Wicca in the period between Catholic and nonreligious either, but that's less relevant to what I'm trying to get at re: religious trauma.

I want to emphasize here that atheism wasn't a reaction to trauma for me. It was an accurate description of my beliefs, and I don't assume that atheism implies trauma. My aversion to engaging with Christianity, however, absolutely was a trauma response.

By "engaging with Christianity," I mean learning more about it, like deep dives into church history, minute details of Catholic doctrine, and other comparative religion type study. It's risky to do that when people around you see you as someone who's strayed from the flock.

I don't see myself as ex-Catholic. I don't relate to the sense of betrayal that a lot of people who identify that way describe, when talking about the Catholic church. I was years away from realizing I wasn't straight or cis, and I don't feel like the church failed me. It just wasn't right for me.

I also didn't stop being an atheist due to finding a religious community that I liked, or vice versa. Those two processes were completely separate and unrelated for me.

I stopped describing myself as atheist not because of a change in my metaphysical beliefs but because I wanted to emphasize different things. I went from "atheist: the existence of god(s) is irrelevant to me" to "agnostic: existence of god(s) are irrelevant and unknowable to me." The reason?

The reason was that I took a philosophy of religion class, as part of the philosophy degree that I was studying for. It was taught so well that my classmates had fervent arguments with each other about whether the professor had taught with a bias towards theism, atheism or agnosticism.

Philosophically, I feel that agnostic is a better description of me than atheist. I have never had a "there is no god" position, and I ultimately prefer to emphasize my position of "this is unknowable through reasoning" over emphasis that I'm not philosophically a theist.

Outside the context of Doing Philosophy of Religion, asking me how I align myself wrt atheism, agnosticism and theism, I shrug and say "it's complicated." And, it depends on whether you consider things like Spinozan pantheism, panentheism and process theology to *be* theism.

I make the Doing Philosophy distinction because I have no interest in arguing that the sorts of theism I find personally compelling are true. It's like, I'm philosophically agnostic but culturally theistic.

Several years after this shift, I decided to convert to Judaism.

I started engaging with Judaism at college because Hillel's monthly shabbat dinners had free food. I kept going back because I loved that the whole liturgy was sung. The melody they used for Adon Olam slapped, and it's still my favorite song in Jewish liturgy.

I could feel safe engaging with Judaism at college because Judaism is non-proselytizing, and many researchers categorize it as non-creedal as well. My conversion process began when I befriended two trans rabbis at a conference, who saw my interest in Judaism and introduced me to more ways of being Jewish than I'd previously known existed. Including ways of being Jewish that I saw as possible for me.

I met queer and nonbinary Orthodox Jews. I met antizionist and nonzionist Jews. I met Jews who were atheists. I encountered Reconstructionist Jews.

Ultimately what drew me deeper into Judaism was the far greater emphasis on tikkun olam (healing the world) than on metaphysical beliefs. And Judaism *loves* arguments about rules. Judaism fits my *shrug*theism, philosophical agnosticism and argumentative nature extremely well.

But back to antitheism. I think that if I didn't have a devoutly Catholic best friend in high school who fully accepted me as atheist and validated my experience of Catholicism not being for me... I might have become antitheist or anti-religion.

Healing my religious trauma required encountering religious people who didn't see the existence of atheists, or of people deciding to leave their religion, as inherently threatening. I needed to encounter Catholics who saw atheists raised Catholic as part of religious diversity.

In order to heal, it was necessary for me to meet religious people who value atheism and apostasy as part of the rich tapestry of human religious experience.

And I think that's why it felt important to message this particular antitheist. To be the kind of religious person I needed, to someone else.

I expected them to end up unfollowing me. I only wish them well, and that they come to understand through encountering other religious people that a world where organized religion is not coercive and peaceably coexists with atheists and nonreligious people is not impossible but very possible.

Being the kind of religious person I needed (to accept my atheism without question) when I was younger to this other person was also important to me, I think, because this was a fellow LGBTQ person, and... I don't think I've ever heard of an LGBTQ-accepting church that demonized nonbelievers.

I hope this person finds an accepting community wherever they are, and finds peace and healing, whatever form that takes for them.
